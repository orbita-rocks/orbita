# The State of Distributed Key-Value Stores, ~2007–2026: What Changed Since Dynamo

## TL;DR
- **The center of gravity moved from leaderless AP to strongly-consistent, range-sharded, consensus-replicated designs.** The dominant modern pattern is "multi-Raft" — a keyspace split into range-based shards, each its own Raft/Paxos group, with an LSM engine (RocksDB/Pebble) underneath and MVCC/HLC on top. Dynamo's leaderless-quorum + conflict-resolution model survives mainly where extreme write availability trumps correctness, and even its originators retreated: DynamoDB uses MultiPaxos, not Dynamo's quorums.
- **Three genuinely new architectural axes appeared:** storage/compute disaggregation onto object storage (S3-backed LSMs like SlateDB; S3 Express One Zone), the shared-log-as-database pattern (Corfu/Delos/LogDevice), and a correctness-engineering culture shift (deterministic simulation testing from FoundationDB → TigerBeetle → Antithesis; TLA+/P at AWS; Jepsen). Cheap tight clock sync (AWS TimeSync, low-double-digit-microsecond accuracy) is quietly making Spanner-style TrueTime approaches commodity.
- **Your Dynamo-era intuitions are now actively misleading in three places:** (1) "strong consistency is too expensive at scale" — multi-Raft + modern hardware made it the default, not the exception; (2) "eventual consistency + client merge is the price of availability" — most teams found the application-level burden intolerable and moved to serializable transactions; (3) "the KV engine is a B-tree" — the LSM tree won decisively as the default, though write-amplification concerns keep B-tree/hybrid alternatives alive.

## Key Findings

1. **Amazon itself walked away from Dynamo.** DynamoDB (2012, papers 2022/2023) shares almost nothing with the 2007 Dynamo paper: it uses MultiPaxos for replication, is multi-tenant and fully managed, and explicitly prioritizes *predictable* single-digit-ms performance over the operational self-management that made Dynamo painful. The 2022 USENIX ATC paper (Elhemali et al.) is the canonical primary source.
2. **The industry converged on multi-Raft / range-based sharding** (TiKV, CockroachDB, YugabyteDB, and now even ScyllaDB "tablets"). Range sharding (not consistent hashing of individual keys) won because it's scan-friendly and splits/merges are metadata operations, not data moves.
3. **The LSM tree is the default storage engine**, with RocksDB (and Go rewrites like Pebble) as the reference. But leveled compaction's write amplification (16–25× measured) drives real counter-movements: tiered/universal compaction, and B-tree engines (redb, LMDB) where reads dominate.
4. **Object-storage-backed KV is the live frontier.** SlateDB, and the general "put the LSM/log on S3" pattern, trades 50–100ms write latency and per-request cost for near-infinite durability and zero-disk operations. S3 Express One Zone (2023, big 2025 price cuts) is the enabling primitive.
5. **Correctness practice changed permanently.** Deterministic simulation testing (DST), pioneered at FoundationDB, is now table stakes for serious new systems (TigerBeetle, and via Antithesis's deterministic hypervisor, retrofitted to etcd). This is arguably the single biggest *cultural* shift since Dynamo.
6. **Redis fragmented.** The 2024 license change (BSD → SSPL/RSAL) triggered the Linux Foundation Valkey fork backed by AWS/Google/Oracle; Redis added AGPLv3 as a third option in Redis 8 (GA May 2, 2025). Meanwhile Dragonfly and Microsoft's Garnet attack Redis's single-threaded ceiling.

## Details

### 1. The major architectural shifts since Dynamo

#### 1.1 From leaderless AP to consensus-replicated strong consistency

The single largest shift is philosophical: the field decided that **eventual consistency pushes an unacceptable burden onto application developers.** Dynamo's model — sloppy quorums, vector clocks, and *application-level* conflict resolution (siblings in Riak) — worked for Amazon's shopping cart but proved painful in general practice. Conflict resolution logic is hard to write correctly, tail latency under read-repair is unpredictable, and reasoning about anomalies is beyond most application teams.

The decisive evidence is Amazon's own trajectory. **DynamoDB is not Dynamo.** As Murat Demirbas summarizes and the 2022 USENIX ATC paper ("Amazon DynamoDB: A Scalable, Predictably Performant, and Fully Managed NoSQL Database Service," Elhemali et al.) confirms: "DynamoDB uses MultiPaxos for replication." Dynamo was single-tenant and forced every team to become database operators; DynamoDB is multi-tenant, fully managed, and its guiding tenet is *predictability* — low single-digit-millisecond latency for a 1KB item, regardless of scale. The follow-on 2023 paper adds transactions, deliberately implemented with update-in-place (no MVCC) to preserve single-key predictability. During the 66-hour 2021 Prime Day event, Amazon systems "made trillions of API calls to DynamoDB, peaking at 89.2 million requests per second, while experiencing high availability with single-digit millisecond performance."

Cassandra — the most prominent surviving Dynamo descendant — has spent a decade adding the strong-consistency primitives Dynamo rejected: lightweight transactions via Paxos (2.0, September 2013), Paxos v2 (4.1), and now the Accord leaderless-transaction protocol (CEP-15) targeting strict-serializable multi-partition transactions. ScyllaDB (C++ Cassandra rewrite) moved cluster metadata onto Raft and replaced vnodes with dynamically-balanced tablets. The whole lineage is migrating *toward* the consistency guarantees Dynamo gave up.

#### 1.2 LSM trees as default; the write-amplification counter-movement

RocksDB (Facebook's fork of Google's LevelDB) became the near-universal embedded engine. CockroachDB's decision to build on RocksDB — and then to *replace* it with Pebble, a Go rewrite — is instructive about both the dominance and the discontents. Cockroach cited RocksDB's sprawl (350k+ LOC vs LevelDB's original 30k), serious compaction bugs, and the C++/Go FFI barrier. Pebble became the default in CockroachDB v20.2 (Nov 2020) and adds LSM-specific optimizations (L0 sublevels, range-deletion-aware compaction, compaction/flush pacing to avoid RocksDB's write stalls).

The core LSM tension is **write amplification**. Per the REMIX paper (Zhong et al., arXiv:2010.12734), "LevelDB and RocksDB adopt the leveled compaction strategy, which leads to high WA ratios of 16.1 and 25.6, respectively," versus 9.26 for tiered PebblesDB and 4.88 for RemixDB. This is the axis:
- **Leveled compaction** (RocksDB default): low read/space amplification, high write amplification. Latency-critical reads.
- **Tiered / size-tiered**: high write throughput, low WA, worse read/space amplification. Ingest-heavy.
- **Universal**: a tiered variant balancing space.

Where reads dominate or write-amp is intolerable, **B-tree engines persist**: LMDB (mmap'd copy-on-write B+tree), and in Rust, redb (pure-Rust, lmdb-inspired copy-on-write B+trees). The Rust ecosystem has produced a wave: fjall (LSM, forbid-unsafe Rust, built on an `lsm-tree` crate), sled (beta), redb. These matter to you specifically as embeddable substrates (§2).

#### 1.3 Storage/compute disaggregation onto object storage

This is the most architecturally novel category and the one closest to your lakehouse world. The thesis, articulated by SlateDB's authors (Chris Riccomini, Rohan Desai): *what does an LSM tree look like if all writes go to object storage?* Object storage gives bottomless capacity, 11-nines durability, and trivial replication — at the cost of 50–100ms latency and per-request pricing (~$0.40/million GET, ~$5/million PUT, per SlateDB's own analysis). SlateDB (Rust, embedded, Apache-2.0) batches writes into SSTs on S3, persists its `DbState` in a manifest updated via compare-and-swap, and uses writer epochs/fencing to prevent zombie writers. Its authors note it is "already used in production by Dropbox, ZeroFS, HelixDB, Opendata and others," and it is explicitly designed "for use cases that are tolerant to 50-100ms write latency." A telling design point from the SlateDB FAQ: they first tried implementing RocksDB's API over S3 and it did not work — "the object store laws of physics don't constrain systems that rely on local disks in the same way."

The enabling hardware primitive is **S3 Express One Zone** (re:Invent 2023): single-AZ directory buckets, consistent single-digit-ms latency, up to 2M reads/200k writes per second per directory bucket. Crucially, object stores are adding compare-and-swap preconditions (S3, GCS, R2, Tigris), which is what makes them usable as coordination substrates. April 10, 2025 price cuts (85% on GET, 55% on PUT, 31% on storage) materially changed the economics. The tradeoff remains: Express One Zone is roughly $0.11–0.16/GB-month (~5–7× S3 Standard), single-AZ (no multi-AZ durability), viable only when latency directly drives business value.

This pattern is spreading: Neon and Aurora disaggregate the log/page store from compute; WarpStream did it for Kafka; the broader "regional durability from object storage, latency from a thin caching tier" idea is now a design template rather than an experiment.

#### 1.4 Shared-log architectures — the log *is* the database

Corfu → Scalog → LogDevice → **Delos** is the key lineage. Delos (Meta, OSDI 2020, Best Paper — Balakrishnan et al.) is the most important recent contribution: it stores Meta's control-plane state as a replicated state machine over a shared log, but its real innovation is **virtualizing consensus**. The VirtualLog chains together "Loglets" (pluggable ordering protocols — MultiPaxos, Raft, ZooKeeper/HDFS-backed, or a native implementation), so you can change the consensus protocol *underneath a running system without downtime*. A MetaStore (a versioned register supporting a conditional write) handles reconfiguration; the Loglet itself needs only a highly-available `seal` operation, not full fault-tolerant consensus — "A seal bit does not require fault-tolerant consensus." Delos went to production in ~8 months and later hot-swapped from a ZooKeeper-backed Loglet to a native one, with a measured latency drop and no downtime. The lesson: separating the *data plane* (ordering) from the *control plane* (leader election, membership, reconfiguration) is a powerful decomposition, and a shared log is a strong primitive for consistency.

#### 1.5 Modern hardware: what actually changed designs vs. stayed academic

- **NVMe + io_uring: real and decisive.** io_uring (Linux 5.1, 2019) enabled near-zero-syscall async I/O. TigerBeetle and Dragonfly are built around it. This changed real systems.
- **Thread-per-core / shared-nothing: real.** ScyllaDB (Seastar), Dragonfly, and Garnet reflect the reality that on modern many-core machines, lock contention and cross-core coordination — not raw CPU — are the bottleneck.
- **Persistent memory (Optane): commercially dead.** Intel wound down Optane in 2022. A large body of PMEM-optimized KV research was stranded; this is the clearest case of hardware killing a research direction.
- **RDMA / kernel bypass (SPDK/DPDK): real but niche.** Dominant in HPC and inside hyperscaler datacenters (and in LLM KV-cache transfer, §5), less so in general-purpose KV stores exposed to customers.
- **CXL / disaggregated memory: mostly still emerging.** Real products exist but production KV designs built on CXL memory pooling are still early; treat claims here as forward-looking.
- **Tight clock sync: quietly transformative** (see §3.4).

**FoundationDB** deserves special emphasis for your purposes. It's an ordered, strictly-serializable KV store with an *unbundled* architecture (separate in-memory transaction system, distributed storage, and Active Disk Paxos coordination/control plane), MVCC + optimistic concurrency (reads never block writes), and a deliberately minimal feature set designed to have "Layers" built on top. Its transaction limits are hard architectural constraints: **5-second transaction window, 10MB transaction size, 10KB keys / 100KB values** — design your transaction boundaries around these from day one. It is the substrate under Apple CloudKit (via the Record Layer), **Snowflake's transactional metadata store**, and Deno. If you're backing an Iceberg-style catalog, FDB is the most battle-tested "transactional KV substrate you build catalogs on" that exists — Snowflake's use is a direct precedent.

**Embeddable engines that matter as building blocks:** RocksDB (C++ reference), Pebble (Go, Cockroach), sled/redb/fjall (Rust), LMDB (C, read-optimized B+tree), SlateDB (Rust, object-storage). For a Python-native/Rust-backend team, the Rust set is the relevant menu: redb for read-heavy/B-tree semantics and minimal write-amp; fjall for safe-Rust LSM with a BTreeMap-like API and MVCC snapshots; SlateDB for object-storage-native durability. (SlateDB ships Rust core with Go, Java, Node, and Python bindings — directly relevant to a Python-native platform.)

**Metadata/catalog KV for lakehouses.** The Iceberg REST Catalog spec (introduced Iceberg 0.14, 2022) decoupled the catalog protocol from its backing store — the backend can be Postgres/JDBC (dev-scale; "no credential vending, no RBAC, no real multi-tenancy"), or a production service like Apache Polaris (incubating since 2024) or Nessie. The core atomicity requirement (compare-and-swap on the current-metadata pointer, for ACID table commits) is exactly a KV problem. At the largest scale, the pattern is either a transactional KV substrate (FoundationDB, as Snowflake does) or a relational DB behind the REST service. This is precisely the design space your platform lives in.

### 3. Consensus and replication protocol evolution

#### 3.1 Multi-Raft / range sharding as the dominant pattern

TiKV is the clearest reference implementation. Data is split into **Regions** (range-based key intervals; historically ~96MB default), each a Raft group of (typically) 3 replicas. A **Placement Driver (PD)** — the term borrowed explicitly from Spanner — is the control plane: it maintains the routing table and acts as scheduler, initiating rebalancing via Raft configuration changes because nodes are "almost stateless" and cannot migrate data autonomously. Regions split when they exceed a size threshold and merge when small; the split itself is committed as a Raft log entry so the operation is safely replicated. This is now the template copied by CockroachDB (ranges + leaseholders) and YugabyteDB (tablets). Note the paper's own caution: "comparing Multi-Raft to Raft is not at all like comparing Multi-Paxos to Paxos" — multi-Raft just means managing many independent Raft groups per node, which is a substantial engineering feat (TiKV batches the event-loop tick across all groups and multiplexes one inter-node connection).

The **operational realities** are the hard part and where Dynamo intuitions fail productively:
- **Leaseholders/leaders concentrate load.** A single leader per range serves reads (via leases) and coordinates writes; hot ranges create hot leaseholders. Systems add leaseholder rebalancing and load-based splitting.
- **Rebalancing is continuous**, not an add-node event, and moves real data (unlike consistent-hashing ring changes which move token ownership cheaply).
- **Hot ranges** (e.g., sequential-key insert workloads) are the classic anti-pattern — the modern analog of Dynamo hot partitions, but the mitigation is load-based range splitting rather than virtual nodes.

**ScyllaDB's "tablets"** are the most recent convert to this model. ScyllaDB 6.0 (June 2024) made Raft the default for schema *and* topology metadata and shipped tablets as a replacement for the Cassandra-inherited vnode scheme. Per ScyllaDB's engineering blog, "the Tablets approach dynamically distributes each table to a subset of nodes and shards based on its size," where "a tablet represents a contiguous token range owned by a group of replicas and shards" that "get dynamically split or merged on demand" (targeting ~5GB geometric-mean size; split at 10GB, merge at 2.5GB). Critically, "tablet replicas live on a particular shard on a given node, which will allow us to bind Raft groups to tablets" — i.e., they are moving toward per-tablet Raft, the same architecture as TiKV/YugabyteDB. This is a striking convergence: even the flagship AP wide-column store is adopting range-based, Raft-coordinated sharding.

#### 3.2 Leaderless consensus revivals — and their difficulties

EPaxos (2013) promised leaderless, commit-in-one-RTT-if-no-conflicts consensus, where any replica can coordinate and non-conflicting commands commit on a fast path. The **"EPaxos Revisited" paper (Tollman, Park, Ousterhout, NSDI 2021)** is the essential corrective: EPaxos achieves optimal *median* WAN commit latency but has **significantly worse tail latency under high-conflict workloads** than originally reported, because the fast path fails and the dependency-resolution slow path (plus conflict-graph analysis before execution) dominates. Their proposed TOQ (timestamp-ordered queuing) optimization itself adds latency and, combined with the "thrifty" optimization, can worsen tails when a single node fails (a timeout+retry is needed to form the fast quorum). Independent re-evaluations reproduce the pattern: at 100% conflict rate EPaxos execution latency degrades to ≥780ms in the Atlas evaluation, making it "unpractical" there. The practical takeaway: leaderless dependency-tracking protocols are fragile precisely where you'd want them, and the fast-path quorum can be *larger* than a simple majority.

**Accord (Cassandra, CEP-15)** is the most serious attempt to make leaderless consensus production-grade. Designed by a team including Apple engineers, it's leaderless and timestamp-ordered (HLC), with two key mechanisms: a **reorder buffer** ("measures the difference between nodes in addition to the latency between them," letting each replica correctly order data and get one-RTT consensus) and **fast-path electorates** (maintaining a leaderless fast path to quorum under the same minority failures Cassandra already tolerates, avoiding the leader-election latency spikes of leader-based protocols). Its authors claim it is "the first leaderless protocol that is sufficiently stable for practical use in a large scale industrial database system," and that unlike Tempo it needs no periodic broadcast for timestamp stability. Critically, Accord depends on CEP-21 Transactional Cluster Metadata (TCM), which replaces Cassandra's eventually-consistent Gossip with a strongly-consistent metadata log — itself a marker of the AP→CP shift. **Status caveat:** as of mid-2026, Accord/Cassandra 6.0 is still in *alpha* (6.0-alpha1 landed ~March 2026); the production line is 5.0.x. It is not yet GA — treat "Cassandra has leaderless ACID transactions" as *imminent, not shipped.*

Related recent protocols: **Tempo** and **Atlas** (EPaxos descendants with smaller quorums / early commit even when conflict reports don't exactly match); **Detock** (deterministic, geo-distributed); **Caesar**. Most remain research or single-vendor.

#### 3.3 Flexible quorums, witnesses, leases

The Flexible Paxos insight (read quorum + write quorum need only *intersect*, not both be majorities) is now widely exploited. Production uses: **witness/non-voting replicas** (holding no data, just participating in votes — cheaper geo-redundancy; CockroachDB, and TigerBeetle's VSR + flexible-quorum design), **learners** (etcd/Raft — catch-up replicas that don't vote), and **leader/leaseholder leases** (time-bounded leadership enabling local reads without a consensus round — universal in CockroachDB/TiKV; TiKV's default lease is derived from the election timeout, ~10s). Quorum-lease refinements extend this to allow local linearizable reads at multiple replicas, not just the leader.

#### 3.4 Geo-distribution and the clock-sync revolution

Spanner (2012) needed TrueTime — GPS/atomic clocks giving bounded uncertainty and *commit-wait*. The imitators avoided special hardware: **CockroachDB uses HLCs + a static max-offset (default 500ms)** and pays for it with **uncertainty restarts** (a read that sees a value within the uncertainty window must restart at a higher timestamp to preserve linearizability). YugabyteDB similarly uses HLCs, explicitly "Raft consensus without any atomic clocks."

The live change: **cheap, tight clock sync is now commodity.** AWS TimeSync with the Nitro Precision Hardware Clock (announced re:Invent 2023) is designed to "deliver clock accuracy in the low double-digit microsecond range," with an exposed error bound (ClockBound); independent testing has measured ~24–52µs, and YugabyteDB reports a "~50 microsecond clockbound." A 2024 Lund University master's thesis ("Utilizing highly synchronized clocks in distributed databases," Jacob & Fabian) modified CockroachDB to consume ClockBound and reported that "uncertainty intervals shrank by a factor of over 500... with maximum bounds of 1.4 milliseconds versus the default 500 milliseconds" — though it also found ClockBound reads cost ~50µs versus ~60ns for a raw system-clock read, forcing them to port the ClockBound daemon into a Go library ("TrueClock") to embed it. YugabyteDB published parallel production integration work; Meta's Precision Time Protocol effort pushes the same direction; Google now exposes a TrueTime API in Cloud Spanner. **The architectural implication:** the thing that made Spanner special (tight, bounded clocks) is becoming a commodity cloud primitive, which makes commit-wait models viable without Google's infrastructure and narrows the gap between the "TrueTime camp" and the "HLC camp." This is one of the most consequential quiet shifts in the field. (Caution flagged by Cockroach Labs: clock error bounds can be violated by VM live-migration pauses and OS scheduling stalls, so operators still need safety margins.)

#### 3.5 Deterministic databases (Calvin lineage)

Calvin's idea — agree a global order of transactions *first*, then execute deterministically (no 2PC) — went into production at **FaunaDB**, and lives on in **Detock** (deterministic geo-distributed). The approach never became the mainstream pattern the way multi-Raft did; it constrains the programming model (transactions' read/write sets must be known in advance, or reconnaissance-executed). It's a real but minority branch. Fauna's own commercial trajectory (the company wound down its database service in 2025) is a caution about the model's market traction, though the *technique* is sound and Detock shows continued research interest.

### 4. Correctness and verification practice — the cultural shift

This is, as the query rightly frames it, a genuine change in engineering standards, and it deserves emphasis because it's easy to miss if you're tracking architecture but not process.

- **Jepsen (Kyle Kingsbury)** made black-box consistency testing under partition a public standard. A bad Jepsen report became reputationally serious; "passes Jepsen" became table stakes. This shifted the industry from vendor consistency claims to *tested* claims.
- **Deterministic Simulation Testing (DST)** is the deeper shift. **FoundationDB** pioneered it: the entire database runs single-threaded in a discrete-event simulation with all I/O, network, and clocks mocked behind interfaces (the `g_network`/`INetwork` swap), driven by a seeded PRNG (`deterministicRandom()`), with fault injection (BUGGIFY). Determinism means every bug is perfectly reproducible. FDB's Record Layer paper reports running "more than 250 million simulations equivalent to more than 1870 years and 3.5 million CPU-hours." This is why FDB has the correctness reputation it does — and note DST and AWS's approach were roughly simultaneous inventions around 2010.
- **TigerBeetle** took FDB's approach as a founding principle (Viewstamped Replication + an LSM-forest storage engine, all deterministic, fuzzed with network *and* storage-fault injection — misdirected/corrupt reads and writes), and made "TigerStyle" (static allocation, determinism, io_uring, zero-deserialization) an explicit methodology. Kyle Kingsbury has publicly praised its VSR + flexible-quorum + protocol-aware-recovery design.
- **Antithesis** (founded by ex-FoundationDB engineers) generalized DST into a **deterministic hypervisor** ("the Determinator") that runs *any* x86 binary under deterministic simulation with fault injection and autonomous, coverage-guided state-space exploration. This removes the "you must build determinism in from day one" barrier — you can retrofit DST onto existing systems. In 2025 the etcd project used Antithesis and, across "830 wall-clock hours of testing, which simulated 4.5 years of usage," found "a critical watch bug... present in all stable releases of etcd" (3.4, 3.5, 3.6) that existing tests had missed.
- **madsim** brings the same deterministic-runtime idea to the Rust async ecosystem (used by RisingWave and others); **Stateright** brings model-checking to Rust.
- **Formal methods went mainstream at AWS.** TLA+ is used to model-check designs (the "How AWS Uses Formal Methods" CACM paper documents catching serious bugs in DynamoDB and S3 before production). AWS then built and open-sourced **P** (a state-machine language for asynchronous systems), used across S3, DynamoDB, MemoryDB, Aurora, EBS, EC2, and IoT — and used it to validate S3's new strong-consistency feature. AWS also applied **lightweight formal methods** (executable reference models + conformance checking, integrated into the build/test framework) to validate S3's ShardStore KV storage node (SOSP 2021, "Using Lightweight Formal Methods to Validate a Key-Value Storage Node in Amazon S3").

The through-line: the field stopped trusting "we tested it and it seems fine" for distributed correctness, and adopted reproducible, adversarial, and formal techniques. For a founder building a data platform, this is now an expectation for infrastructure you'd bet on.

### 5. What's actually new and interesting (2024–2026)

- **Object-storage-native databases are the hottest area.** SlateDB, the "S3 as the disk" pattern, and the WarpStream (Kafka-on-S3, acquired by Confluent 2024) / Neon (serverless Postgres, acquired by Databricks 2025) style disaggregation. The open design question: how thin can the latency-serving tier be, and can object-store CAS preconditions serve as the coordination primitive? This is the most active frontier and the one most relevant to a serverless Iceberg platform. Note also AWS S3 Tables (re:Invent 2024) — the first cloud object store with native Iceberg support and managed compaction — which productizes exactly this convergence of object storage and table metadata.
- **Serverless / multi-tenant KV.** DynamoDB's multi-tenant fairness/admission-control lessons (2022 paper — bursting, adaptive capacity, per-partition throughput isolation) are now widely studied; per-tenant isolation on shared infrastructure is a live topic.
- **Clock-sync-enabled designs** (§3.4): the collapse of uncertainty intervals via commodity microsecond clocks is being actively exploited (CockroachDB, YugabyteDB integrations 2024–2025).
- **Disambiguation: "KV store" vs "LLM KV cache."** These are unrelated despite the name collision, and the reader should hold them separately. The **LLM KV cache** is the per-token attention key/value tensors cached during transformer inference; systems like **Mooncake** (Moonshot AI's Kimi platform; arXiv 2024, FAST 2025, ACM ToS 2024/25), **LMCache**, and disaggregation work store/tier these across GPU HBM, DRAM, SSD, and even object storage (ObjectCache), moving them with RDMA. Mooncake reports operating "across thousands of nodes, processing over 100 billion tokens daily" and increasing effective request capacity 59–498% under SLOs. This is a *storage-systems* problem and borrows techniques (tiering, disaggregation, CXL) from the KV-store world, but it is **not** a key-value database. Where the two genuinely touch: (a) both push on disaggregated memory/CXL, and (b) vector-search indexes (for RAG) are increasingly built on or beside KV stores — Cassandra 5.0 added a vector type + HNSW ANN index built on its Storage-Attached Indexes (CEP-30), and Redis 8 added vector sets (designed by antirez). That "KV store as substrate for vector indexes" pressure is real and worth watching.
- **Redis fragmentation as an ecosystem event.** The timeline: BSD (through 7.2.4) → SSPL/RSALv2 (March 2024) → AGPLv3 added as a third license option in Redis 8 (announced May 1, GA May 2, 2025). The Linux Foundation forked 7.2.4 into **Valkey** (BSD, backed by AWS/Google/Oracle/Ericsson/Snap) within days of the SSPL move; Percona reported 83% of large enterprises adopting or exploring Valkey by September 2024. Redis's reversal followed antirez's return (Nov 2024), who conceded "SSPL, in practical terms, failed to be accepted by the community." Meanwhile **Dragonfly** (BSL 1.1, source-available — *not* OSI-open) and Microsoft's **Garnet** (MIT, .NET, Tsavorite log) attack Redis's single-threaded-execution ceiling with shared-nothing thread-per-core designs.
- **Live debates:** Is leaderless consensus (Accord) worth its complexity vs. multi-Raft? Does the object-storage-latency floor (even with Express One Zone) permanently bar S3-backed stores from OLTP, or will CAS + a caching tier close it? Has commodity clock sync made HLC-uncertainty designs obsolete in favor of commit-wait? These are unresolved and actively argued at SOSP/OSDI/VLDB/CIDR.

## Recommendations

A staged decision framework, oriented to your context (Python-native serverless Iceberg platform; Go/Rust backend; K8s).

**Step 1 — Classify the workload on the axes that actually matter (in priority order):**
1. **Consistency/transaction needs.** Do you need multi-key serializable transactions, or is single-key atomicity + idempotency enough? This is the biggest fork. If you need serializable multi-key txns, you're in FoundationDB / TiKV / CockroachDB-KV territory. If single-key suffices, the menu is much wider (DynamoDB, embedded engines).
2. **Embedded vs. service.** A library in your process (RocksDB, Pebble, redb, fjall, SlateDB) vs. a distributed service (TiKV, FDB, DynamoDB). Embedded eliminates a network hop and an operational surface but ties storage to your deployment unit.
3. **Deployment/operational model.** Self-hosted on K8s vs. managed. Multi-Raft systems (TiKV/CockroachDB) are operationally non-trivial (rebalancing, hot ranges, compaction tuning). If you don't want to operate consensus, use managed (DynamoDB) or push durability to S3 (SlateDB).
4. **Cost model.** Per-request (object-storage-backed, DynamoDB on-demand) vs. provisioned instances (self-hosted). Object-storage-backed inverts the cost curve: cheap at rest, per-request at write — which is why SlateDB batches writes to amortize PUT costs.
5. **Geo-distribution.** If multi-region strong consistency matters, you need HLC-uncertainty (Cockroach/Yugabyte) or, increasingly, commodity clock-sync integration. If not, don't pay for it.

**Step 2 — For an Iceberg catalog / metadata backend specifically:** the atomic compare-and-swap on a metadata pointer is a transactional-KV problem. Precedent strongly favors either (a) **FoundationDB** as the transactional substrate (Snowflake's metadata store is the direct proof point, and FDB's ordered-KV + Layers model is purpose-built for this), or (b) a **REST Catalog (Polaris/Nessie) over Postgres** for moderate scale, migrating the backend later without touching client configs. If you want cloud-native economics and can tolerate the latency floor, **SlateDB-style object-storage-backed KV** is the frontier-but-promising option and philosophically aligned with your Iceberg-on-S3 architecture (and it ships Python bindings). Watch object-store CAS-precondition maturity as the gating factor, and watch AWS S3 Tables as a managed alternative to rolling your own.

**Step 3 — For embedded/single-node needs** (local state, caching, spill, per-worker indexes): default to **RocksDB/Pebble** for write-heavy LSM workloads with a mature ecosystem; **redb** or **LMDB** for read-heavy/B-tree-semantics and minimal write-amp; **fjall** if you want safe-Rust LSM; **SlateDB** if you want object-storage durability without local disks.

**Step 4 — Demand modern correctness evidence.** For any infrastructure you'd bet the company on, treat **DST and/or a public Jepsen report as table stakes**, not a bonus. FoundationDB, TigerBeetle, and (via Antithesis) etcd set the bar. This is a cheap filter that would have caught many historical data-loss incidents.

**Benchmarks/thresholds that would change these recommendations:**
- If p99 write latency budget is < ~10ms, **rule out object-storage-backed stores** (SlateDB's own guidance: 50–100ms write tolerance).
- If sustained write amplification is starving your IOPS budget, **switch leveled → tiered/universal compaction** (moving from a ~16–25× to a ~5–9× WA regime) or move to a B-tree engine for that dataset.
- If you're doing sequential-key inserts and see a hot range/leaseholder, **enable load-based splitting** or hash-prefix the keys — the modern hot-partition fix.
- If cross-region uncertainty restarts dominate CockroachDB/Yugabyte latency, **integrate AWS TimeSync/ClockBound** to collapse the uncertainty interval (>500× demonstrated, 500ms → ~1.4ms).

## Caveats and uncertainties

- **Vendor benchmarks are not independent.** ScyllaDB's "7.2× faster scaling than Cassandra" and Dragonfly's "25× Redis throughput" are vendor-sourced; directionally plausible (both attack real single-threaded/vnode bottlenecks) but not neutral measurements. Treat them as claims, not data.
- **Cassandra 6.0 / Accord is not GA** as of mid-2026 — it's in alpha (6.0-alpha1 ~March 2026; production line 5.0.x). Statements that "Cassandra has leaderless ACID transactions" describe an imminent capability, not a shipped one. This conclusion is robustly multi-sourced (Apache repo/dev list, The New Stack, Instaclustr, AxonOps); exact alpha dates rest partly on one detailed independent tracker.
- **Redis licensing is a moving target and remains multi-license:** BSD (≤7.2.4), SSPL/RSALv2 (from March 2024), and AGPLv3 added in Redis 8 (May 2025) as a third option — a tri-license, not a clean revert. Valkey (Linux Foundation, BSD) is the fully-permissive fork; Dragonfly uses BSL 1.1 (source-available, not OSI-open). Verify the current license against your compliance needs before adopting.
- **CXL/disaggregated memory and persistent memory:** PMEM/Optane is commercially dead (Intel exited 2022); CXL production KV designs are still early — claims here are forward-looking.
- **The AP→CP narrative has limits.** Leaderless AP remains the right choice for genuine extreme-write-availability, latency-insensitive, conflict-tolerant workloads (telemetry ingest, some caching). The shift is that this is now the *exception* requiring justification, not the default. Dynamo-era intuitions about consistent hashing, sloppy quorums, and CRDT merge are still correct *within that niche* — they're just no longer the mainstream, and they're actively misleading if applied to a general-purpose transactional store.
- **Sourcing thinness:** some system-specific version/date details (ScyllaDB tablet sizing thresholds, Cassandra alpha dates, FaunaDB's wind-down) rest on vendor blogs or single independent trackers; I've flagged these inline. Where a precise claim matters for a decision, confirm against the primary release notes.
